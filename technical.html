How the overbounce actually works

This page goes into the actual code behind the overbounce. If you just want to know how to pull one off, the main page has that. This one is for people who want to know exactly what the game is doing wrong and why.

Where it lives

The bug sits inside PM_StepSlideMove, part of the player movement code that handles stairs and ramps. Its whole job is to let you walk up a step or a slope without the game treating it like a wall. When you're on the ground and moving into something steep enough to cut your movement short, the game tries a step-up: it traces upward, slides you along the new surface, then traces back down to see where you'd actually land. That's the mechanism that makes stairs and ramps feel smooth instead of bumping you to a stop.

There's no specific slope angle that triggers a step-up attempt. It happens under two conditions instead: either your movement bumped into something and got redirected at all, or you were already on the ground and whatever you hit blocked more than 10% of where you were trying to go. Most slopes and stairs steep enough to actually feel like an obstacle will trip one of those two, which is why it feels tied to slope angle in practice, even though no angle value is ever checked in the code.

The scale factor that never gets clamped

Once the step-up/step-down resolves, the game measures how much your vertical position had to be corrected to make it work, and folds that into a scale factor applied to your entire velocity vector: 

absDelta  = fabs(origin.z_after - origin.z_before)   // how far the step-up/down moved you
scalePred = (1.0 - absDelta / stepHeight) * 0.8 + 0.2
velocity.x *= scalePred
velocity.y *= scalePred
velocity.z *= scalePred

stepHeight is 18 units normally, or 10 while prone. The intent is obvious once you read the formula: a small correction should barely touch your velocity (scalePred close to 1), a correction close to a full step height should slow you down hard (scalePred close to 0.2). It's a soft brake, and for the vast majority of normal steps and ramps, that's exactly what it does.

The problem is that nothing stops absDelta from exceeding stepHeight by a lot. Once absDelta / stepHeight passes 1.25, scalePred goes negative. There's no max(0, scalePred) anywhere, so the formula just keeps going into negative territory and gets applied as-is. Multiplying a negative velocity (you falling, velocity.z < 0) by a negative scale gives a positive result: instead of braking your fall, the game launches you upward, roughly in proportion to how far past the threshold you landed.

In concrete numbers, that means a vertical correction bigger than 22.5 units while standing or crouched, or 12.5 units while prone, is enough to flip the sign. Land on the edge of a slope after a real fall, one that would normally produce a correction that large, and you get a rebound instead of a stop.

Two passes, not one (in the edge-landing case)

For the edge-landing technique described on the main page, the flip isn't a single event, it's two consecutive frames both running the same correction. Here's an actual logged sequence at 15 FPS, vertical speed only: 

frame A:  -2128  ->  -1461   (feet touch the edge, scalePred still positive, a normal brake)
frame B:  -1461  ->  +1610   (still counted as grounded, scalePred goes negative, the flip)

The first frame is an ordinary step correction, the kind that happens constantly and is completely harmless: your feet clip the edge, the correction is still within range, and you get slowed down the way the formula intends. But that contact leaves you flagged as grounded for one more frame. On the next frame the correction runs again, except now the geometry has shifted enough that the correction is bigger, past the threshold, and this time the scale factor is negative. That's the frame that actually launches you. So in this example, the number right before the launch is not the raw fall speed, it's already the output of the first, harmless correction. This two-step pattern is what we've observed with the edge-landing setup specifically, nothing in the formula itself requires two frames. Land with a large enough correction on the very first contact and the flip could in principle happen in one, we just haven't logged a case of that.

Why framerate matters

The correction that feeds into absDelta comes out of the physics steps that happen earlier in the same frame, and how far you move in one of those steps is your velocity multiplied by the frame's time step. A lower framerate means a bigger time step, which means a bigger chunk of movement gets resolved in a single frame, which means a bigger position correction for the exact same fall speed. That's the entire reason dropping to a low FPS changes things so much: it's not changing anything about the collision logic, it's just making each individual frame's correction bigger, so the same fall speed crosses the 22.5-unit threshold that it wouldn't cross at a smooth 125 FPS. Fall fast enough and you'll cross the threshold at any framerate, but low FPS is what makes it achievable at fall speeds you can realistically reach. Landing the setup itself, on the exact edge, at the right moment, is still the hard part.

Why prone almost never works

Right before the scale factor gets applied, PM_StepSlideMove calls PM_VerifyPronePosition, which checks whether your current posture is valid. For standing and crouching, that function does nothing at all, it returns immediately without checking anything. It only actually runs a check when a specific posture flag is set, and that flag corresponds to prone specifically, not crouch. Standing and crouching share the exact same code path here.

When it does run, in prone, it hands off to BG_CheckProne, which traces your torso and pelvis against the world and checks the resulting angle against a valid range. If that check fails, the entire frame gets rolled back: your origin and velocity both get reset to what they were before the step-up was ever attempted, and the scale factor calculation never happens. No bounce, no bug.

The correction big enough to trigger the bug in the first place tends to leave your character in a position that doesn't read as valid prone geometry, so BG_CheckProne fails almost every time right when it would matter most. That's a plausible explanation for why the bug is so much harder to get prone rather than something structurally blocking it. Nothing in the code specifically targets prone, it's just collateral damage from a geometry check that exists for an unrelated reason.

Not a one-off mistake

Whatever else is going on in PM_StepSlideMove, this particular formula isn't an isolated slip. Earlier in the same function, before the scale factor is ever computed, there's a dot-product test that rejects steps that didn't actually gain any ground, a separate check against your jump budget, and a prone-position check, all of which exist specifically to catch bad step results. The scale factor itself just doesn't get the same treatment: nothing clamps it once it's computed.

This isn't just a CoD4 problem either. The same unclamped scale factor exists in other Call of Duty titles built on this engine, confirmed on Black Ops 1. It's a mistake that got carried forward across several games rather than something introduced specifically in CoD4.
